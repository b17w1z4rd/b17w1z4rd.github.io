Shashank Patil · 93+ machines across HackTheBox and TryHackMe

Ninety-plus boxes is a lot of nmap. This post isn't a walkthrough of any single one — it's what the volume actually bought me, because the exploits are the least transferable thing you take away from a machine. The transferable thing is a sense of where people hide their mistakes.

$ what the reps are for

Everyone starts the same way: a VPN config, a target IP, and the slow realisation that nmap -sV is not the whole job. It's barely the first ten minutes of it. The early boxes teach mechanics — enumerate services, find the version, match it to a known exploit, get a shell, escalate. That loop is necessary and it is also not the point.

The point shows up somewhere around box forty, when the mechanics have gone quiet enough that you stop asking "what's the CVE" and start asking a better question: what did the person who built this assume, and where did they stop checking? That reframing is the whole return on the grind. A service running as root that didn't need to be. A backup script with an unquoted wildcard. A credential that crosses a trust boundary because someone was tired on a Friday. After enough machines, those stop being individual tricks and become a single instinct for where the soft spot is.

Volume beats cleverness early on. The ninetieth box teaches you less than the tenth — but you cannot get to the tenth without grinding through the first nine. Grind first, get selective later.

$ a methodology that survived the volume

The rough shape I settled into, box after box: 

1. enumerate everything, then enumerate again — the foothold
   is usually in the service you dismissed on the first pass
2. understand the app before attacking it; know why the bug
   exists, not just that a script exists for it
3. get the least-privilege foothold, then treat the box as a
   fresh enumeration problem from the inside
4. escalate on misconfiguration and trust, not just kernel CVEs
5. write down what actually cost you time — that's the note
   worth keeping

Step one is the one beginners skip and everyone else swears by. The foothold is almost always in the thing you glanced at and moved past — the odd port, the unlabelled vhost, the service you assumed was default. "Enumerate again" has unstuck more of my boxes than any single exploit.

$ the ones that stuck

A handful earned permanent residence in memory — Fulcrum, FluxCapacitor, Ariekei, Node, Shrek among them. They're the ones that broke my assumptions rather than rewarding them: boxes where the obvious path was a dead end, where the real route ran through a layer I'd written off, where "the perimeter" turned out to be a lie because there was always another network, another container, another thing behind the thing.

The specific solves belong in their own writeups — the kind with the actual commands and the actual dead ends, which is the only kind worth reading. Those are coming, one box at a time. This post is the map; the terrain follows.

$ the part nobody advertises

The reason this all mattered later: every offensive technique on these boxes has a signature in the logs. Learning to move quietly through a machine is also learning exactly what "quiet" looks like from the defender's chair — and that turned out to be the foundation for the detection work I care about now. You can't write a good detection for an attack you've never had to perform. The boxes were where I performed them.
